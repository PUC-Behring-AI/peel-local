Expertise, opacity, and trust in AI systems Éloïse Boisseau 1 Introduction The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics in social epistemology (Hardwig, 1985, 1991; Lackey, 2018; Goldman, 2001; Rolin, 2002; Whyte & Crease, 2010). An overview of some of the major questions surrounding expertise further reveals the extent to which this notion of deference hinges on more general relationships of ‘epistemic dependence’, to use John Hardwig’s (1985) expression. Our ability to gain knowledge, presented as an irreducibly social enterprise, is arguably one in which it is then rational for a layperson to rely on the testimony of the expert (Hardwig, 1985)1. This rational reliance on the expert to gain knowledge is nevertheless associated with a certain sort of (more or less) voluntary blindness from the one seeking knowledge. As Hardwig (1991, p. 693) pointed out, trust is in essence ‘partially blind’ (emphasis added). Gran ting trust to gain knowledge or make an informed decision implies, at least temporarily and figuratively, to shut one’s eyes and rely on some thing other than one’s own cognitive, perceptive or intellectual capacities. These matters about trust, dependence and expertise in the context of knowledge acquisition, traditional as they are, are nowadays being revisited as our epistemic world is being redefined by the arrival of what might seem to be new epistemic agents: artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities. Used in an ever-increasing number of contexts and based on deep (or multilayer) artificial neural net works (DNNs), the programs we are ref erring to are so-called ‘learning’ programs, based on the method known as ‘deep learning’. These programs are proving to be extremely versatile: depending on the data they are ‘trained’ on, they can carry out tasks (hitherto exclusively reserved to human experts) with a certain degree of autonomy and in a generally highly satisfactory manner. A network trained to detect patterns within an initial data set can thus be mobilised to discover trends within this corpus and be able, when subsequently confronted with a novel item, to make predictions, take decisions, propose diagnoses, categorise, etc., on its basis. Systems involving deep learning are widely used (to mention only some of the cases most frequently dis cussed in the literature) in the context of medical diagnosis and imaging (Lee et al., 2017; Grote & Berens, 2020; Bjerring & Busch, 2020), pol icing – within what is sometimes referred to as ‘predictive pol icing’ – (Asaro, 2019; Walsh, 2022), banking, finance and insurance (Narang et al., 2024; Pingili, 2024). The observation is therefore that a growing number of our hitherto highly specialised tasks are now falling under the yoke of an epistemic dependence not only on human experts, but also on these new systems the efficiency and precision of which are often equivalent to, or greater than, that of human experts (Esteva et al., 2017; Rajpurkar et al., 2017). It is also widely suggested that a new stage has in fact been reached as far as epistemic dependence is concerned, due to the possession by these programs of an unprecedented characteristic for artificial systems: a kind of ‘opacity’ (Burrell, 2016; Creel, 2020; Ross, 2022; Zerilli, 2022; Duede, 2022). In an influential paper, Jenna Burrell (2016, pp. 1–2) distinguishes three main forms of opacity for nonexperts, with regard to the functioning of machine learning-based AI systems. The first one arises as a result of industrial secrecy (‘opacity as intentional corporate or institutional self-protection and concealment’). The second one is presented as a form of ‘technical illiteracy’ or ‘technical opacity’ – to use two labels that have been given to it in the literature, respectively by Grote and Berens (2020) and Kaminski (2018). This form of opacity, Burrell explains, ‘stem[s] from the current state of affairs where writing (and reading) code is a specialist skill’. Finally the third form of opacity Burrell identifies is the unintelligibility to humans of the mathematical processes involved in an algorithm. This, she writes, ‘stems from the mismatch between mathematical optimization in high dimensionality characteristic of machine learning and the demands of human-scale reasoning and styles of semantic interpretation’. Burrell’s three forms of opacity overlap to a certain extent with four varieties which we shall consider below. Opaque systems are furthermore frequently referred to in the literature as epistemic ‘black boxes’ (London, 2019; Creel, 2020), insofar as it is virtually impossible to verify or obtain any kind of explanation or justification concerning the production of a particular output. Black box systems, in other words (those of Frank Pasquale, 2015, p. 3), are systems ‘whose workings are mysterious’. The use of such ‘opaque’ systems naturally poses a number of ethical problems, including embarrassing cases of ‘responsibility gaps’ (Coeckelbergh, 2020; Da Silva, 2024) and algorithmic biases (Walsh, 2022; Walker et al., 2023) – the latter being liable to lead the considered technology to be described as ‘oppressive’ (Huebner & Liao, 2021). Given that these risks are real and relatively well identified, a pressing question then arises as to what precise account or expression should the trust placed in such systems be given. A somewhat recurrent hypothesis in the literature – sometimes stated explicitly, other times implicitly or underlyingly – is that the trust placed in these opaque systems should be modelled on the type of trust we ordinarily place in human experts. The rationale would be that the epistemic dependence to which we are subject in relation to deep learning-based systems would itself be similar or analogous to that which we experience in relatively ordinary situations of lay deference to the expert (Zerilli et al. 2019, London, 2019). The reason for this comparison would lie in the existence of a similar form of opacity, manifest in both cases. One can read, for example, that ‘essentially, the relationship between practitioner and AI system is epistemic ally analogous to the relationship between practitioner and expert’ (Bjerring & Bush, 2020, p. 6), or that ‘the features that make expert opinion trustworthy, and our trust in those individuals’ decisions both epistemic ally and ethically responsible—can be realized in AI as well’ (Ross, 2022, p. 8). According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature (Grote & Berens, 2020). The output produced by the program would then, in these contexts, be regarded as an epistemic judgement in its own right, and the issue of peer disagreement would arise in an equivalent manner in cases where the judgements of two human experts differ and in cases where the output of a program and the judgement of a human expert differ (Grote & Berens, 2020). In what follows, I shall argue that there are few merits but many limits to this comparison between the human expert and the artificial system. The second section of the paper will offer a brief reflection on the trust ordinarily placed in epistemic instruments and the upheaval caused in this respect by current AI systems. The third section of the paper will set out some arguments in favour of conceptualising our trust in artificial systems on the model of our trust in human experts, as they are sometimes found in the literature. We shall see that this conceptualisation is based on the much dis cussed but ambiguous notion of opacity (which in fact crystallises many debates surrounding artificial decision-making systems). An examination of such arguments will furthermore reveal the underlying conception of expertise on which the conceptualisation stands, which is veritistic through and through. The fourth section of the paper will show the limits of this conception of expertise, and will argue against conceptualising trust in artificial systems on the model of trust in human experts. I will temper the extent to which human expertise is opaque, and suggest a number of criteria for being deemed ‘expert’ on a subject matter – which, as we shall see, AI systems logically cannot satisfy. I will conclude by suggesting that AI systems cannot be genuine ‘experts’ and thus cannot literally be ‘epistemic peers’ with which human experts might agree or disagree. Therefore, modelling the trust we place in AI systems on the trust we place in human experts is a misguided (and potentially epistemic ally dangerous) attitude. Indeed it is only once we recognise that these systems are at best instruments (and not epistemic collaborators or superiors) that we can begin to reflect fruitfully on the appropriate epistemic behaviours to adopt regarding them. 2 Trust in artificial systems 2.1 Trust and reliance Let us first make a few distinctions regarding this notion of trust visàvis artificial systems. For a start and from a general perspective, one crucial feature of trusting another agent is often presented as a form of uncertainty coupled to a positive expectation towards the trusted agent (Danks, 2019). Uncertainty on the one hand, because trusting implies not knowing for sure that the trusted agent will as a matter of fact be trustworthy. And a positive expectation on the other hand, as trusting another agent suggests that one does think that said agent is, as a matter of fact, reliable or trustworthy. These two characteristics (uncertainty and positive expectation) are also tied to a form of vulnerability on the part of the agent who takes the step of placing their trust in another. We can read, e.g. in Danks (2019, p. 521), that ‘at a high level, we often understand trust as a relation in which the trustor makes oneself vulnerable based on positive expectations about the behavior or intentions of the trustee’ (see also Alvarado, 2022). Turning now more specifically to trust in an artificial system (an artefact), let us point out the following: it is traditional to distinguish at least between two aspects of trust in artificial systems: the most ‘traditional’ aspect (since it initially concerns machines in the traditional sense of the term – i.e. artefacts with moving parts) is that of ‘reliance’ (Coeckelbergh, 2012). Recently, a more debatable aspect to come forth in the context of trust in artefacts is that of the ‘trustworthiness’ of a given artificial system. With regard to the first aspect, trust as a form of reliance reflects the idea that one can count on a specific artefact (one can rely on it) to function properly. This aspect partly (but not entirely) coincides with what Ramón Alvarado (2022, p. 4) considers to be a ‘technical approach to trust in AI’, and to what Mark Ryan (2020) coins a ‘rational account’ of trust (see also Nickel, 2013). In other words, by using a specific artefact, one expects it to behave or function in a certain way (namely in the way that was intended at the time of its design) and thus to enable one to do certain things (those for which it was precisely designed). Discussions about epistemic artefacts often take the time to distinguish more finely between several types of justification for the trust (as reliance) placed in these instruments. Following the categorisation pro posed by Rom Harré (2010) – recently revisited by Eamon Duede (2022) – we can distinguish between physically mediated instruments and theoretically mediated instruments (Duede, 2022) – two types of epistemic instruments with two types of justifications for the trust placed in them. The former, namely physically mediated instruments, have a causal relationship with the world they enable us to study (a paradigmatic example would be the telescope). The confidence that one is justified in placing in them depends in part on the prior establishment of ‘the limits and conditions under which the instruments function with consistency’, and on the subsequent assurance that they are indeed used within these limits and under the right conditions (Duede, 2022, p. 9). The latter, i.e. theoretically mediated instruments – which are of particular interest to us here – entertain a relationship that is no longer causal but purely conceptual with the world they enable us to study (the paradigmatic example being the model or simulation). The confidence one is justified in placing in them rests this time on ‘knowledge of the principles that the procedure operationalizes’, as well as on knowledge of the conditions ‘under which these principles apply’ (ibid. p. 10)6. In relation to artefacts or machines, the positive expectation of the user therefore reflects a form of epistemic deference towards the agents who created the artefacts: someone using a GPS device to find their way around an unfamiliar city does not need to know how the device works in order to rely on it. All they need to do is accept that it has been properly configured by individuals who, for their part, understand the ‘principles that the procedure operationalizes’ (to use Duede’s wording) – i.e. the principles that govern it. The trust that users place in their GPS (their reliance on its proper functioning) is then understood as what Mark Coeckelbergh (2012, p. 55) ultimately describes as a merely predictive trust. This trust is based on the prima facie trust that users place in the people (the experts) who created the system. Trust in artefacts is thus, as Coeckelbergh writes (p. 54), a form of ‘indirect trust in the humans related to the technology’. 2.2 Trust and trustworthiness As regards the second aspect of trust – i.e. trust as a recognition of the trustworthiness of another agent – the matter is to some extent analogous to that of mere reliance, but it is also frequently considered to be a much more demanding characteristic (and one that it is not obvious that a machine can possess). A commonplace observation regarding trustworthiness is, of course, that there may be a discrepancy between an agent’s decision to trust another and the latter’s actual trustworthiness. In other words, it is conceivable (and in fact rather common) for one to trust someone who is not actually trustworthy, and conversely not to trust a person who is, as a matter of fact, trustworthy. Trustworthiness is considered to be much more meaningful than mere reliability, and being trustworthy would require the recipient of trust to possess particular moral characteristics. Trust is therefore sometimes referred to as a type of ‘affective attitude’ (Jones, 1996; see also Baier, 1994) insofar as the trustee would be motivated by a form of goodwill towards the trustor: they would not want to disappoint or let them down. On the other hand, the trustor – as Karen Jones explains in her well-known paper (1996, p. 4) – would demonstrate ‘an attitude of optimism about [the trustee’s] goodwill and […] have the confident expectation that, when the need arises, the one trusted will be directly and favorably moved by the thought that [one is] counting on her’. Another widely echoed approach to trusting someone is that of trust as a normative attitude (see particularly Baier, 1986). As Richard Holton (1994, p. 67) explains it, the normative account of trust implies that ‘[w]hen you trust someone to do some thing, you rely on them to do it, and you regard that reliance in a certain way: you have a readiness to feel betrayal should it be disappointed, and gratitude should it be upheld’. Thus, according to these accounts of trust as an affective or normative attitude, it is clear that an artificial system is not the kind of thing that could be the recipient of trust in this sense. In other words, such a system could not logically be trustworthy, as ‘trustworthiness can only be a property of “full moral agents”’ (Ryan, 2020, p. 10). On this point Ryan agrees with Jones (1996, p. 14), who remarks that … Trusting is not an attitude that we can adopt toward machinery. I can rely on my computer not to destroy important documents or on my old car to get me from A to B, but my old car is reliable rather than trustworthy. One can only trust things that have wills (…) Several authors now consider that trust in the latest AI systems (based on deep learning) – although it can no longer be thought of solely in terms of reliance and needs to be reconceptualised – should not be approached in the same way as one approaches interpersonal trust, i.e. trust between human agents (Ryan, 2020; Alvarado, 2022). Here, I shall be in broad agreement with this type of views, which can be subsumed under the label – coined by Ori Freiman (2022, p. 4) – of ‘anthropocentric views of trust’. In Freiman’s words, this label designates (…) several different approaches to trust, [which] all commonly share two features: (a) not ascribing human agency to technologies; and (b) ref erring to humans behind the technologies in matters regarding trust and responsibility. (Freiman, ibid.) To outline this general point, I shall take as a case study a family of positions in the literature on trust in AI systems that, on the contrary, specifically rely on attributing a form of expertise to AI systems by comparing certain characteristics of these systems with certain supposedly analogous characteristics of human experts. 3 Blind trust and expertise 3.1 Let us meet in the dark We have already noted that there is a constitutive form of (partial) blindness in trust. However, a significant subset of the philosophical and epistemological literature on AI systems takes up this observation to put forward the following hypothesis: the epistemic position we find ourselves in (as nonspecialists in a domain) when it comes to assessing the correctness and reliability of the judgements of a human expert would actually be no more advantageous than the position we find ourselves in when it comes to assessing the correctness and reliability of the judgements of an AI system (Zerilli et al. 2019; London, 2019). The reason often put forward lies in the existence of a supposed analogous opacity, manifest in both cases. Indeed, when one places trust in a human expert, this trust is very rarely based on a form of direct verification of the correctness or validity of the epistemic operations carried out by the expert in question. There is usually no attempt on the part of the non specialist to verify the experts’ assertions for themselves. On the contrary, they are arguably rationally justified in defer ring to the experts’ decisions without having access to the reasoning behind them. There is thus a form of blindness and obscurity in the trust placed in human experts. It is precisely this supposed similarity in blindness that has led some authors, whose views I wish to challenge here, to hypothesise that we are in fact in quite similar situations when we interact with human experts and AI systems. Let us take a look at a few concrete examples of this rhetoric. Amber Ross (2022), in a recent paper, goes in this direction and advocates the fertility of such a conceptualisation of our relationship with AI systems by analogy with the relationship we ordinarily have with human experts. She writes that … our relation to some opaque AI technology may be sufficiently similar to the ordinary layperson’s relation to the specialized expert such that analyzing the successful expert/layperson epistemic relation may provide us with a blueprint for how to best utilize opaque AI, both practically and ethically. (Ross, 2022, p. 2) The opacity of AI systems is therefore no longer seen solely as an epistemic impediment to be overcome, but also as part of the solution to this very impediment.

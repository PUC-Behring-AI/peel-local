Expertise, opacity, and trust in AI systems form a complex relationship rooted in social epistemology. The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics. An overview of the major questions surrounding expertise further reveals that this notion of deference hinges on relationships of 'epistemic dependence,' to use John Hardwig's expression. Our ability to gain knowledge is a social enterprise, and while we often rely on the testimony of the expert for rational acquisition of this knowledge, there is an associated voluntary blindness from the one seeking knowledge. As Hardwig (1985, 1991) pointed out, trust is in essence 'partially blind.' When our epistemic world is being redefined by new epistemic agents: artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities, this reliance on testimony becomes critical. We shall see that the conceptions of expertise and trust underlying current literature are based on much discussed but ambiguous notions of opacity, which crystallize many debates surrounding artificial decision-making systems.

An influential hypothesis suggests trusting these opaque systems on the model of trust in human experts. The rationale would be that the epistemic dependence to deep learning-based systems would be similar or analogous to ordinary lay deference. One can read that the relationship between practitioner and AI system is epistemic ally analogous to the relationship between practitioner and expert (Bjerring & Busch, 2020). According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature. The output produced by the program would then be regarded as an epistemic judgement in its own right, leading to issues of peer disagreement equivalent to those between two human experts (Grote & Berens, 2020). However, this analogy rests on a specific conception of expertise known as 'veritistic' or truth-linked. This approach considers expertise primarily as the possession by an agent of extensive knowledge and skills, often characterized as true belief (Goldman, 1991; Watson, 2018).

The veritistic conception helps bring to light necessary characteristics of expertise, such as consistency in reaching the truth and producing knowledge. Some literature also emphasizes the relationship between acquisition of knowledge and understanding. It is argued that an expert must understand substantial proportions of terms, propositions, arguments, and procedures (Watson, 2018). Furthermore, another necessary condition for attributing 'expert' status to an individual involves the possibility of giving reasons in support of one's judgement. This implies a normative field where reasons interact with correct and incorrect outcomes (Cockburn, 2001; Lowy, 2009). While opacity of human expert reasoning is often described as an inability to explain decisions due to complex or hidden mental processes, it is crucial to distinguish between 'reasons' (normative justifications) and 'causes' (mechanical physical processes). The opacity of the brain's neural operations is a cause, not a reason. To attribute this operational blindness to AI systems would conflate causes with reasons, ignoring the distinction that explanations for actions are normative while causal mechanisms are descriptive (Hacker, 2007; Lowe, 2009).

Another significant aspect of human expertise involves its institutional or social dimension. The layperson's trust is largely based on epistemic institutions providing certification or authentication of expertise (Goldman, 1985). Expertise possesses both a social character (the badge of credibility) and a personal (or intellectual) character. These include moral and intellectual qualities such as honesty, conscientiousness, attentiveness, and non-bias. These are aspects of 'intellectual character' or virtues that enable an expert to make decisions judiciously. The status of expert is acquired, not innate, requiring an individual to demonstrate skills and be recognized for their competence (Whyte & Crease, 2010). Consequently, if AI systems were considered experts in this sense, they would lack the objective intellectual character and moral qualities necessary for true expertise, failing to qualify them as 'epistemic peers' with human beings. This creates a twofold risk: placing too much trust can lead to false results or loss of capacity for critical thinking, while attributing responsibility to the machine absolves humans (Ryan, 2020; Allen & Wallach, 2009). Therefore, modelling the trust we place in AI systems on human experts is a misguided attitude that fails to recognize their lack of intellectual character. It is only once we recognize these systems as instruments rather than collaborators or superiors that we can reflect fruitfully on the appropriate epistemic behaviors to adopt regarding them (Boisseau).

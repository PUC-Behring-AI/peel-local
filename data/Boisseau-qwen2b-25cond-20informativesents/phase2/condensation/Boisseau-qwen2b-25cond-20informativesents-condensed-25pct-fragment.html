Expertise, opacity, and trust in AI systems

Éloïse Boisseau

Expertise, opacity, and trust in AI systems form a complex relationship rooted in social epistemology. The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics. An overview of the major questions surrounding expertise further reveals that this notion of deference hinges on relationships of 'epistemic dependence,' to use John Hardwig's expression. Our ability to gain knowledge is a social enterprise, and while we often rely on the testimony of the expert for rational acquisition of this knowledge, there is an associated voluntary blindness from the one seeking knowledge. As Hardwig (1985, 1991) pointed out, trust is in essence 'partially blind.' When our epistemic world is being redefined by new epistemic agents: artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities, this reliance on testimony becomes critical. We shall see that the conceptions of expertise and trust underlying current literature are based on much discussed but ambiguous notions of opacity, which crystallize many debates surrounding artificial decision-making systems.

Our ability to gain knowledge, presented as an irreducibly social enterprise, is arguably one in which it is then rational for a layperson to rely on the testimony of the expert (Hardwig, 1985)1.

This rational reliance on the expert to gain knowledge is nevertheless associated with a certain sort of (more or less) voluntary blindness from the one seeking knowledge.

Gran ting trust to gain knowledge or make an informed decision implies, at least temporarily and figuratively, to shut one’s eyes and rely on some thing other than one’s own cognitive, perceptive or intellectual capacities.

This blackbox rhetoric, as it is used by the authors mentioned above, seems to invite us to distinguish several varieties of opacity that are supposedly immanent to the relationship of trust between the layperson and the expert – an opacity generally presented, as we have just seen, as a place of reunion for the human expert and the AI system on which we rely to make decisions or acquire knowledge.

The expert is thus characterised by this dual relationship with a field of knowledge, on the one hand, and an epistemic society on the other hand: experts are those who are particularly well informed on a given subject (Coady, 2012; Goldman, 2001, 2018; Scholz, 2009) and who play a very specific practical social role, namely that of epistemic resource for other individuals (Coady, 2012; Goldman, 2018; Croce, 2019).

An expert would be anyone or any thing that could reliably enable the attainment of truth or the acquisition of knowledge for other individuals.

We have, of course, pointed out in the foregoing that there is sometimes a form of opacity with regard to the ability of experts to justify their judgements or decisions (and that it is precisely on this point that the comparison of the AI system with the human expert is based).

We can thus understand that the expert in whom we place our trust is expected not only to have a good track record – and therefore to be reliable – in matters of truth or the acquisition of knowledge, but also to have formed their beliefs in a good way.

The honesty of the individual who claims the status of expert in terms of their understanding of an object of study, the requirement for a non superficial knowledge of the more general field within which their research falls, the ability to justify their decisions, actions or diagnoses using relevant reasons, and now their various specific moral and intellectual qualities make up what can be described as the ethos of the expert, i.e. their intellectual character.

2 Trust in artificial systems 2.1 Trust and reliance Let us first make a few distinctions regarding this notion of trust visàvis artificial systems.

To outline this general point, I shall take as a case study a family of positions in the literature on trust in AI systems that, on the contrary, specifically rely on attributing a form of expertise to AI systems by comparing certain characteristics of these systems with certain supposedly analogous characteristics of human experts.

Despite this disparity in the varieties of opacity, one conclusion seems to emerge (from these different fronts): that opacity – as a characteristic common to AI systems and human experts – can certainly be apprehended in a uniform manner when trust is granted to humans and when trust is granted to AI systems.

Show 4 more source(s)

Consequently, the problem now appears to be to determine how the question of the opacity of human experts’ decisions is usually dealt with and resolved, and then to apply a similar or equivalent recipe to solve that of the opacity of artificial decision-making systems.

I will suggest in what follows that only the social dimension of expertise can possibly be applied to artificial systems.

We could indeed very well imagine people being trained to recognise certain characteristics that have been designated upstream as qualifying criteria for expertise in artificial systems.

The conceptualisation of the relationship of trust between humans (lay people or specialists) and artificial systems on the model of the relationship between lay people and experts (or between experts visàvis each other) poses a twofold risk: on the one hand, of course, there is the risk of placing too much trust in these systems, which could lead to false results being accepted as true, or bad solutions being accepted as good ones.

An influential hypothesis suggests trusting these opaque systems on the model of trust in human experts. The rationale would be that the epistemic dependence to deep learning-based systems would be similar or analogous to ordinary lay deference. One can read that the relationship between practitioner and AI system is epistemic ally analogous to the relationship between practitioner and expert (Bjerring & Busch, 2020). According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature. The output produced by the program would then be regarded as an epistemic judgement in its own right, leading to issues of peer disagreement equivalent to those between two human experts (Grote & Berens, 2020). However, this analogy rests on a specific conception of expertise known as 'veritistic' or truth-linked. This approach considers expertise primarily as the possession by an agent of extensive knowledge and skills, often characterized as true belief (Goldman, 1991; Watson, 2018).

A veritistic conception of expertise A first point to note concerning our conception of expertise and the possibility of its attribution to an artificial system is that we frequently consider it from an approach that can easily be described, following Alvin Goldman (1991, 2001, 2018) as ‘veritistic’ or ‘truth-linked’.

According to this conception of expertise – which is only broadly outlined here –, the expert is someone who, for a given field or subject, finds themselves in a particular epistemic position – i.e. one that dominates – in relation to the vast majority of other epistemic agents.

If a person repeatedly proves to be a reliable source of knowledge in a field, then the non specialist seems a priori justified in defer ring to their judgement – and this conception of expertise is then said to be ‘reliabilist’ (Sosa, 1991, 2017; Zagzebski, 1996).

Taken in a sense that could probably be described as ‘functionalist’ (Croce, 2019), such a veritistic conception of expertise does not in fact seem to reserve the category of candidates for the title of ‘expert’ to human beings.

In what follows, in contrast, I shall try to show why this conception of expertise is reductive and that the idea put forward by Grote and Berens – for instance – of an artificial system as an expert and an epistemic peer, must face some vexing objections.

It could be argued here that this whole conception of expertise (which requires a capacity for understanding on the part of the person who is to be called an ‘expert’) is overly anthropocentric.

However, this analogy is unsatisfactory insofar as the opacity of the expert’s reasoning is in no way equivalent to the opacity of algorithmic processes, for the following reason: opacity in a human context is only partial and contingent, whereas it is, on the contrary, complete and necessary in the case of artificial systems.

One distinction to bear in mind, however, is that while our artificial systems may not act rationally or morally on their own, they are nevertheless designed to act in accordance with a certain conception of rationality and morality.

Subsequently, I attempted to show how the veritistic conception of expertise is inadequate, and how other factors need to be taken into account before a person can be considered an expert.

Indeed, some thing that still today regularly stands out in the literature on expertise (and that is still of importance when it comes to discus sing the expertise of AI systems) is the insistence, in some of the definitions, on the relationship between the agent described as an ‘expert’ and – on the one hand – some knowledge they have acquired in a domain (sometimes simply and notoriously characterised, since Plato’s Theaetetus, as a set of ‘true and justified beliefs’ – Goldman, 2001;

Pritchard, 2018; Hetherington, 2019), and – on the other hand – the agent’s capacity to acquire or produce new knowledge in this same domain (Goldman, 2001; Watson, 2018).

So-called ‘reliabilist’ epistemology, since Goldman (1979), insists on the fact that the acquisition of knowledge must be ‘non accidental’ and must involve a process or method that is accordingly ‘reliable’ – the reliability of a procedure being defined here as ‘the tendency of a process to produce beliefs that are true rather than false’ (Goldman, 1979, p. 95).

The veritistic conception helps bring to light necessary characteristics of expertise, such as consistency in reaching the truth and producing knowledge. Some literature also emphasizes the relationship between acquisition of knowledge and understanding. It is argued that an expert must understand substantial proportions of terms, propositions, arguments, and procedures (Watson, 2018). Furthermore, another necessary condition for attributing 'expert' status to an individual involves the possibility of giving reasons in support of one's judgement. This implies a normative field where reasons interact with correct and incorrect outcomes (Cockburn, 2001; Lowy, 2009). While opacity of human expert reasoning is often described as an inability to explain decisions due to complex or hidden mental processes, it is crucial to distinguish between 'reasons' (normative justifications) and 'causes' (mechanical physical processes). The opacity of the brain's neural operations is a cause, not a reason. To attribute this operational blindness to AI systems would conflate causes with reasons, ignoring the distinction that explanations for actions are normative while causal mechanisms are descriptive (Hacker, 2007; Lowe, 2009).

4 Artificial experts versus human experts 4.1 Expertise, comprehension and justification The veritistic conception, although it helps bringing to light some of the necessary characteristics of expertise, suffers from a number of limitations.

In addition to an individual’s consistency in reaching the truth and producing knowledge, other characteristics or qualities are in fact frequently put forward and presented as potentially influencing positively or negatively the trust that people are prepared to place in the person they designate as an expert.

An examination of such arguments will furthermore reveal the underlying conception of expertise on which the conceptualisation stands, which is veritistic through and through.

It is then understandable that the trust placed in the expert in this veritistic conception may be based on what Jennifer Lackey (2018) describes as a ‘Track Record Argument’, which consists in acknowledging the visible superiority of one individual over others in terms of their ability to reach the truth on a given subject.

Taken in a sense that could probably be described as ‘functionalist’ (Croce, 2019), such a veritistic conception of expertise does not in fact seem to reserve the category of candidates for the title of ‘expert’ to human beings.

In doing so, we in fact ignore the distinction between the normative field proper to reasons and the mechanical field proper to causes.

As Richard Holton (1994, p. 67) explains it, the normative account of trust implies that ‘[w]hen you trust someone to do some thing, you rely on them to do it, and you regard that reliance in a certain way: you have a readiness to feel betrayal should it be disappointed, and gratitude should it be upheld’.

A first instance of breakdown in the attribution of the character of ‘expert’ to an individual who nevertheless makes correct judgements on a specific subject may thus take the form of a case where the individual in question expresses themselves or takes a position on a subject about which they actually know and understand nothing.

Indeed, it should be noted that although the practice of an expert sometimes seems indescribable using explicit rules, and although their judgement may sometimes appear to be ‘artistic’ (Shortliffe & Buchanan, 1975, p. 352) or vague, this initial observation should not lead us to conclude that the expert is positioned outside the normative field of reasons.

One distinction to bear in mind, however, is that while our artificial systems may not act rationally or morally on their own, they are nevertheless designed to act in accordance with a certain conception of rationality and morality.

Another significant aspect of human expertise involves its institutional or social dimension. The layperson's trust is largely based on epistemic institutions providing certification or authentication of expertise (Goldman, 1985). Expertise possesses both a social character (the badge of credibility) and a personal (or intellectual) character. These include moral and intellectual qualities such as honesty, conscientiousness, attentiveness, and non-bias. These are aspects of 'intellectual character' or virtues that enable an expert to make decisions judiciously. The status of expert is acquired, not innate, requiring an individual to demonstrate skills and be recognized for their competence (Whyte & Crease, 2010). Consequently, if AI systems were considered experts in this sense, they would lack the objective intellectual character and moral qualities necessary for true expertise, failing to qualify them as 'epistemic peers' with human beings. This creates a twofold risk: placing too much trust can lead to false results or loss of capacity for critical thinking, while attributing responsibility to the machine absolves humans (Ryan, 2020; Allen & Wallach, 2009). Therefore, modelling the trust we place in AI systems on human experts is a misguided attitude that fails to recognize their lack of intellectual character. It is only once we recognize these systems as instruments rather than collaborators or superiors that we can reflect fruitfully on the appropriate epistemic behaviors to adopt regarding them (Boisseau).

However, a significant subset of the philosophical and epistemological literature on AI systems takes up this observation to put forward the following hypothesis: the epistemic position we find ourselves in (as nonspecialists in a domain) when it comes to assessing the correctness and reliability of the judgements of a human expert would actually be no more advantageous than the position we find ourselves in when it comes to assessing the correctness and reliability of the judgements of an AI system (Zerilli et al. 2019; London, 2019).

The idea would be to equip ourselves with an institutional apparatus analogous to the one that is put in place in the context of human expertise, which could take charge of assessing the reliability of an artificial system – and which could also play the role of moral guarantee for our deference towards the decisions taken by a machine.

On the one hand, the title of ‘expert’ is eminently social insofar as it acts as a ‘badge’ (Martin, 1973, p. 159 – cited by Watson, 2021, p. 140) conferred on an individual – a badge which, moreover, enables them to simultaneously be endowed with another qualification: that of ‘credibility’ (Fricker, 1998; Rolin, 2002; Whyte & Crease, 2010).

5 Conclusion My aim in this paper was to assess how far the analogy holds between the trust a layperson places in an expert and the trust – whether from a layperson or an expert – that a human being places in a deep learning-based artificial system.

These matters about trust, dependence and expertise in the context of knowledge acquisition, traditional as they are, are nowadays being revisited as our epistemic world is being redefined by the arrival of what might seem to be new epistemic agents: artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities.

It is also widely suggested that a new stage has in fact been reached as far as epistemic dependence is concerned, due to the possession by these programs of an unprecedented characteristic for artificial systems: a kind of ‘opacity’ (Burrell, 2016; Creel, 2020; Ross, 2022; Zerilli, 2022; Duede, 2022).

According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature (Grote & Berens, 2020).

The output produced by the program would then, in these contexts, be regarded as an epistemic judgement in its own right, and the issue of peer disagreement would arise in an equivalent manner in cases where the judgements of two human experts differ and in cases where the output of a program and the judgement of a human expert differ (Grote & Berens, 2020).

In what follows, I shall argue that there are few merits but many limits to this comparison between the human expert and the artificial system.

The third section of the paper will set out some arguments in favour of conceptualising our trust in artificial systems on the model of our trust in human experts, as they are sometimes found in the literature.

The fourth section of the paper will show the limits of this conception of expertise, and will argue against conceptualising trust in artificial systems on the model of trust in human experts.

Therefore, modelling the trust we place in AI systems on the trust we place in human experts is a misguided (and potentially epistemic ally dangerous) attitude.

Turning now more specifically to trust in an artificial system (an artefact), let us point out the following: it is traditional to distinguish at least between two aspects of trust in artificial systems: the most ‘traditional’ aspect (since it initially concerns machines in the traditional sense of the term – i.e. artefacts with moving parts) is that of ‘reliance’ (Coeckelbergh, 2012).

To outline this general point, I shall take as a case study a family of positions in the literature on trust in AI systems that, on the contrary, specifically rely on attributing a form of expertise to AI systems by comparing certain characteristics of these systems with certain supposedly analogous characteristics of human experts.

They write that … A large part of human-level opacity stems from the fact that human agents are also frequently mistaken about their real (internal) motivations and processing logic, a fact that is often obscured by the ability of human decision makers to invent post hoc rationalizations.

It is commonplace in the literature on the opacity of expert decisions to be reminded that there is a whole range of fields of expertise in which explicit ‘principles’ are simply ‘either elusive or unwelcome’ (Ground, 2024, p. 137).

The fourth variety of opacity that I want to consider here is quite singular insofar as it does not exclusively concern individuals who defer to a decision-making epistemic agent (lay people or nonexperts, in other words).

This form of opacity is reflected in the form of ‘cognitive processes underlying human choices’, which thus differ from the previously mentioned opacity of judgement linked to the non explicit nature of the method followed by an expert (the relational opacity of the expert), and rather designates the presence in decision-making of what are often described as ‘implicit biases’ or ‘unconscious biases’ (Greenwald & Banaji, 1995)11.

Despite this disparity in the varieties of opacity, one conclusion seems to emerge (from these different fronts): that opacity – as a characteristic common to AI systems and human experts – can certainly be apprehended in a uniform manner when trust is granted to humans and when trust is granted to AI systems.

Whether the opacity is contingent or not, whether it is the effect of bias or not, whether it is an effect of perspective or not, whether it is the echo of our ignorance or not, it does seem to be systematically assimilable in the case of the human expert and in the case of the expert software.

Consequently, the problem now appears to be to determine how the question of the opacity of human experts’ decisions is usually dealt with and resolved, and then to apply a similar or equivalent recipe to solve that of the opacity of artificial decision-making systems.

3.2 Expertise, trust and credibility A dimension of expertise and trust placed in human experts is worth mentioning: that it is deeply social or even institutional in nature.

The layperson’s trust in the expert is thus largely based on the existence of a certain number of epistemic institutions that are specifically endowed with the role of certification or authentication of expertise.

The idea would be to equip ourselves with an institutional apparatus analogous to the one that is put in place in the context of human expertise, which could take charge of assessing the reliability of an artificial system – and which could also play the role of moral guarantee for our deference towards the decisions taken by a machine.

Let us now try to outline the con tours of expertise in more detail, in order to understand the extent to which an artificial system cannot claim this status (in its objective dimension – and thus should not be granted it in its subjective one either).

This will allow us to understand more clearly why the analogy between the trust placed in such a system and the trust placed in human experts is unsatisfactory.

A veritistic conception of expertise A first point to note concerning our conception of expertise and the possibility of its attribution to an artificial system is that we frequently consider it from an approach that can easily be described, following Alvin Goldman (1991, 2001, 2018) as ‘veritistic’ or ‘truth-linked’.

According to this conception of expertise – which is only broadly outlined here –, the expert is someone who, for a given field or subject, finds themselves in a particular epistemic position – i.e. one that dominates – in relation to the vast majority of other epistemic agents.

It seems that a conceptual background of this kind regarding the nature of expertise can be found in some of the statements made today in the literature on AI systems used in decision making.

After all, if a practitioner knows of an epistemic source that is both more knowledgeable, more accurate, and more reliable in decision making, she should treat it as an expert and align her verdicts with those of the source.

Since I do not wish to deny the possibility that a human being can acquire knowledge through an artificial system (that would indeed be going too far), let me simply say that Freiman’s notion of ‘AI testimony’ or of ‘technology-based beliefs’ (Freiman, 2024b), as well as Alvarado’s (2023) aforementioned concept of ‘epistemic technology’ can certainly all prove useful for theorising anew about the way human beings acquire knowledge through AI.

My point for now is indeed limited to rejecting the – in my sense, highly problematic – trend of describing some artificial systems as ‘experts’ in the same sense that we would normally describe epistemic ally competent human beings as ‘experts’.

The addition of the criterion of comprehension thus leads us to adopt what Watson (2018, p. 46) characterises as a form of ‘epistemic facility account’ of expertise – which he presents as follows: (EF) A subject, S, is an expert in a subject matter, M, if and only if S (a) understands a substantial proportion of the terms, propositions, and arguments in M, along with the procedures used to formulate propositions in M, and (b) S has the ability to demonstrate (a) successfully in the discharge of her epistemic activities.

However, this analogy is unsatisfactory insofar as the opacity of the expert’s reasoning is in no way equivalent to the opacity of algorithmic processes, for the following reason: opacity in a human context is only partial and contingent, whereas it is, on the contrary, complete and necessary in the case of artificial systems.

There is indeed a great temptation to equate these two types of opacity: the opacity concerning the reasoning followed by the human expert (manifested by their inability to explain their decision) would be directly correlated with the opacity concerning the unintelligibility of the neuronal processes in their brain.

For our purposes, these cursory reflections on the distinction between reasons and causes lead us to the following realisation: while it can certainly be useful, in the case of machine learning systems, to determine the causes that led a program to produce a particular output (and the explanation of the causal mechanisms at play can then, for instance, take the form of an analysis of the weights assigned to the various connections between the artificial neurons of each layer), in the case of human decisions or judgements, on the other hand, it is not by mobilising a cause that we will be able to explain why a particular decision or judgement was put forward.

4.5 Epistemic peers and disagreement These remarks about machines’ lack of intellectual character allow us to return to the questions raised earlier: can artificial systems be experts in the same way that human beings can?

The risk of thinking of artificial systems on the model of human experts (particularly in terms of the trust we place in them) can also take the form of absolving the human agents who use them of their responsibility.

It would go beyond the scope of my remarks to develop a theoretical framework for the kind of trust that should legitimately be placed in artificial systems, or to fully explore the question of responsibility for an outcome for which a machine is involved.

Given that the opacity of a human expert’s actions, decisions, and judgements usually does not prevent lay people from trusting them, this opacity is sometimes invoked to justify placing a similar level of trust in AI systems.

In these contexts, the opacity of deep learning programs is no longer presented as an obstacle to the acquisition of knowledge and to the trust that we are justified in placing in AI, but as a criterion of its expertise.

Expertise, opacity, and trust in AI systems

Éloïse Boisseau

The discourse surrounding expertise, opacity, and trust within artificial intelligence systems has become a central focal point in contemporary social epistemology. The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics in the field, drawing upon a rich theoretical lineage including the works of Hardwig, Lackey, Goldman, Rolin, and Whyte & Crease. An overview of some of the major questions surrounding expertise further reveals the extent to which this notion of deference hinges on more general relationships of epistemic dependence, to use John Hardwig's expression. These matters about trust, dependence, and expertise in the context of knowledge acquisition, traditional as they are, are nowadays being revisited as our epistemic world is being redefined by the arrival of what might seem to be new epistemic agents: artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities. Systems involving deep learning are widely used (to mention only some of the cases most frequently discussed in the literature) in the context of medical diagnosis and imaging, policing – within what is sometimes referred to as predictive policing – banking, finance, and insurance. The use of such opaque systems naturally poses a number of ethical problems, including embarrassing cases of responsibility gaps and algorithmic biases. A somewhat recurrent hypothesis in the literature – sometimes stated explicitly, other times implicitly or underlyingly – is that the trust placed in these opaque systems should be modelled on the type of trust we ordinarily place in human experts. According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature. The second section of the paper offers a brief reflection on the trust ordinarily placed in epistemic instruments and the upheaval caused in this respect by current AI systems. We shall see that this conceptualisation is based on the much discussed but ambiguous notion of opacity, which crystallizes many debates surrounding artificial decision-making systems. The fourth section of the paper will show the limits of this conception of expertise and argue against conceptualizing trust in artificial systems on the model of trust in human experts. Therefore, modelling the trust we place in AI systems on the trust we place in human experts is a misguided attitude that can be potentially epistemically dangerous.

4 Artificial experts versus human experts 4.1 Expertise, comprehension and justification The veritistic conception, although it helps bringing to light some of the necessary characteristics of expertise, suffers from a number of limitations.

Since I do not wish to deny the possibility that a human being can acquire knowledge through an artificial system (that would indeed be going too far), let me simply say that Freiman’s notion of ‘AI testimony’ or of ‘technology-based beliefs’ (Freiman, 2024b), as well as Alvarado’s (2023) aforementioned concept of ‘epistemic technology’ can certainly all prove useful for theorising anew about the way human beings acquire knowledge through AI.

My point for now is indeed limited to rejecting the – in my sense, highly problematic – trend of describing some artificial systems as ‘experts’ in the same sense that we would normally describe epistemic ally competent human beings as ‘experts’.

However, this analogy is unsatisfactory insofar as the opacity of the expert’s reasoning is in no way equivalent to the opacity of algorithmic processes, for the following reason: opacity in a human context is only partial and contingent, whereas it is, on the contrary, complete and necessary in the case of artificial systems.

One distinction to bear in mind, however, is that while our artificial systems may not act rationally or morally on their own, they are nevertheless designed to act in accordance with a certain conception of rationality and morality.

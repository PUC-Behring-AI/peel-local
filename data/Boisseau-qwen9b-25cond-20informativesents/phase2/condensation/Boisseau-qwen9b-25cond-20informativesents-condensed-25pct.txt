Expertise, Opacity, and Trust in AI Systems: A Critique of the Human Analogy

The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics in social epistemology. Philosophers and sociologists have long examined these dynamics, noting that our reliance on others for knowledge acquisition is a fundamental aspect of human existence. This notion of deference hinges on more general relationships of 'epistemic dependence,' to use John Hardwig's expression, where individuals surrender the full burden of verification to those they deem competent. Traditionally, this dynamic was reserved for human agents: doctors diagnosing illnesses, scientists publishing findings, or legal experts interpreting statutes. However, the landscape of our epistemic world is now being radically redefined by the arrival of artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities. These programs are proving to be extremely versatile; depending on the data they are 'trained' on, they can carry out tasks—hitherto exclusively reserved to human experts—with a certain degree of autonomy and in a generally highly satisfactory manner. This shift forces us to revisit traditional matters about trust, dependence, and expertise. We must ask whether these tools function merely as instruments or if they have become bona fide new experts.

Systems involving deep learning are widely used, to mention only some of the cases most frequently discussed in the literature. In the context of medical diagnosis and imaging, algorithms assist physicians in interpreting scans and identifying pathology with remarkable speed and sometimes accuracy. In policing—within what is sometimes referred to as 'predictive policing'—these systems analyze historical data to predict crime hotspots or assess risk. In banking, finance, and insurance, similar models manage credit risk, detect fraud, and optimize portfolios. Finally, the third form of opacity Burrell identifies is the unintelligibility to humans of the mathematical processes involved in an algorithm. Opaque systems are furthermore frequently referred to in the literature as epistemic 'black boxes,' insofar as it is virtually impossible to verify or obtain any kind of explanation or justification concerning the production of a particular output. The use of such 'opaque' systems naturally poses a number of ethical problems, including embarrassing cases of 'responsibility gaps' and algorithmic biases. When an opaque system makes a harmful error, who is responsible? The programmer? The user? The data provider? Or the system itself? These questions have led to debates regarding the nature of agency and moral responsibility.

A somewhat recurrent hypothesis in the literature—sometimes stated explicitly, other times implicitly or underlyingly—is that the trust placed in these opaque systems should be modeled on the type of trust we ordinarily place in human experts. The rationale would be that the epistemic dependence to which we are subject in relation to deep learning-based systems would itself be similar or analogous to that which we experience in relatively ordinary situations of lay deference to the expert. According to these views, the AI system would then be considered a bona fide new expert, no longer human but artificial in nature. This analogy rests on two primary pillars: first, the shared characteristic of opacity between human experts and AI systems; second, the functional success of AI systems, which supposedly warrants trust regardless of internal process transparency. To outline this general point, let us first make a few distinctions regarding this notion of trust vis-à-vis artificial systems. Following the categorization proposed by Rom Harré—recently revisited by Eamon Duede—we can distinguish between physically mediated instruments and theoretically mediated instruments. The trust that users place in their GPS is then understood as what Mark Coeckelbergh ultimately describes as a merely predictive trust. We rely on the device to function correctly, but we do not necessarily understand or care about its internal workings. However, trustworthiness is considered to be much more meaningful than mere reliability, and being trustworthy would require the recipient of trust to possess particular moral characteristics. When we trust a human expert, we are implicitly trusting their virtue: their integrity, their conscientiousness, their commitment to truth. Trust in an instrument is different; it is grounded in past performance and physical mediation rather than epistemic character.

Yet, many proponents of AI as expert argue that deep learning systems have crossed the threshold from mere instruments to genuine epistemic agents. They claim that the reliability (the good track record) of these systems therefore makes them entirely honourable candidates to play the role just mentioned of epistemic resource for human agents. In fact, current AI systems are regularly referred to as 'epistemic technologies.' Alvarado's interesting characterisation mobilised here actually implies an epistemic gradation of artefacts. The functioning of expert systems is indeed solely based on a form of 'mere computation, calculation and memory retrieval' in contrast to which Alvarado defines the truly epistemic nature of the most recent AI systems. This distinction relies on the idea that early AI was dumb, rule-based, and mechanical, while modern deep learning systems operate through vast, complex neural networks that mimic human cognition so closely that they must be granted a form of autonomy. For example, Jens Christian Bjerring and Jacob Busch state that insofar as AI systems will eventually outperform the best practitioners in specific medical domains, practitioners will have epistemic obligation to rely on these systems in medical decision making. They argue that if a tool works better than a human, we should defer to it. This is a purely veritistic and functionalist conception of expertise: truth-getting is all that matters, not the agent's character or intentions. Under this framework, competence is defined narrowly as accuracy. If an AI outputs correct answers 95% of the time, it is deemed competent, regardless of whether it understands what it is doing. This allows for artefacts to act as epistemic experts and hence epistemic peers, insofar as they provide broadly correct results.

However, this veritistic conception, although it helps bringing to light some of the necessary characteristics of expertise, suffers from a number of limitations. It ignores the social and institutional dimensions that constitute real-world expertise. A dimension of expertise and trust placed in human experts is worth mentioning: that it is deeply social or even institutional in nature. The layperson's trust in the expert is thus largely based on the existence of a certain number of epistemic institutions that are specifically endowed with the role of certification or authentication of expertise. Medical boards license doctors; universities confer degrees on professors; peer-reviewed journals validate research findings. These institutions provide badges—socially assigned labels—that signal to us that someone possesses an extensive fund of knowledge and a set of skills or methods for apt and successful deployment of this knowledge to new questions in the domain. Goldman defines the expert in a particular field as someone who possesses such knowledge and skills. But beyond this, expertise involves virtues: virtue epistemology emphasizes the possession and exercise by an agent of particular skills or 'epistemic virtues.' Competence, conscientious work, and epistemic self-assessment are aspects of B's 'epistemic character.' A human expert can confess ignorance, admit error, and revise their view in light of new evidence. An AI system cannot. It has no capacity for humility, no understanding of what it does not know, and no moral agency to respond to ethical concerns. We could indeed very well imagine people being trained to recognise certain characteristics that have been designated upstream as qualifying criteria for expertise in artificial systems. But this is a superficial mimicry; it confuses output with process, result with reasoning.

This leads us directly into the heart of the controversy: opacity. Opacity would constitute an ordinary characteristic of expertise. There are several presentations of this conceptualisation of human reasoning and decision-making processes (which here concern both experts and nonexperts) based on the blackbox model. Ross notes that for the vast majority of society, the evidence and reasoning processes of any expert in a specialized field is opaque, a genuine 'black box.' Neuroscientists argue that much of our own thinking occurs unconsciously. We make decisions without being able to articulate why we made them. John Zerilli et al. suggest that explaining human actions is all the more tricky because the agents themselves are sometimes unaware of their true motivations or what may have played a role in their reasoning. They write that a large part of human-level opacity stems from the fact that human agents are also frequently mistaken about their real (internal) motivations and processing logic, a fact that is often obscured by the ability of human decision makers to invent post hoc rationalizations. To take one example, Evan Thompson et al. rightly point out that expert Go players 'use their finely honed pattern recognition skills, which they cannot verbalize or formulate in terms of explicit concepts and rules.' Their intuition leads them to brilliant moves, yet they cannot explain the heuristic processes behind those moves. They operate at a level of cognition that transcends conscious rule-following. Similarly, AI systems operate beyond human comprehension due to the complexity of their architectures. Yet, there is a temptation to equate these two types of opacity: the opacity concerning the reasoning followed by the human expert (manifested by their inability to explain their decision) would be directly correlated with the opacity concerning the unintelligibility of the neuronal processes in their brain. As Jonathan Lowe noted, the 'seemingly small step' of turning the reasons for a person's action or decision into the causes of their action or decision is in reality 'an erroneous [step] of enormous magnitude.' The confusion at stake rests on a fraudulent assimilation of causes with reasons in the context of the explanation of actions. Where human actions and decisions do require to be explained by reasons, the outputs of AI systems can only be explained by causes (or metonymically by the reasons of their designers and/or users). Reasons are normative; they justify actions based on values and principles. Causes are descriptive; they explain events based on prior states and laws. To say that an AI system has 'reasons' for its decisions is to attribute agency where there is only computation. The fourth variety of opacity that I want to consider here is quite singular insofar as it does not exclusively concern individuals who defer to a decision-making epistemic agent (lay people or nonexperts, in other words). This form of opacity is reflected in the form of 'cognitive processes underlying human choices,' which thus differ from the previously mentioned opacity of judgement linked to the non explicit nature of the method followed by an expert (the relational opacity of the expert), and rather designates the presence in decision-making of what are often described as 'implicit biases' or 'unconscious biases.' For example, a doctor may unconsciously favor patients who resemble them racially or culturally, despite their best intentions. This bias is not necessarily detectable by the doctor themselves and thus contributes to unjust outcomes. AI systems can inherit similar biases from their training data, perpetuating discrimination at scale. Despite this disparity in the varieties of opacity, one conclusion seems to emerge (from these different fronts): that opacity—as a characteristic common to AI systems and human experts—can certainly be apprehended in a uniform manner when trust is granted to humans and when trust is granted to AI systems. Consequently, the problem now appears to be to determine how the question of the opacity of human experts' decisions is usually dealt with and resolved, and then to apply a similar or equivalent recipe to solve that of the opacity of artificial decision-making systems. But here lies the critical divergence: society does not demand full transparency from human experts. We trust them precisely because we acknowledge their fallibility and our dependence on their specialized judgment. We do not hold them hostage to impossible standards of self-explanation that would cripple effective practice. If we apply this standard to AI systems, we may find ourselves rejecting valuable tools simply because we cannot peer inside their black boxes. This is a false analogy; it ignores the crucial distinction between human opacity (which involves moral agency and potential for correction) and technical opacity (which often masks systemic flaws or biases that humans can identify and fix).

The present paper aimed solely at questionning the assumption regularly found in a subset of the literature on AI epistemology that AI systems should (or even could) be considered analogous to human experts, given that we (as nonexperts) supposedly find ourselves in the same opaque position when it comes to evaluating both of their results. I simply wanted to establish that one particular strand of thinking in the literature on the subject (that draws a parallel between the opacity of AI systems and the opacity of human experts) is misguided. It is used by human beings to describe (primarily) certain human beings with certain particular qualities or abilities. Of course, it is debatable to speak of the pro-positional outputs of AI systems as a species of testimony if this type of testimony is precisely devoid of all the necessary properties of testimony. Testimony involves communication between agents who share norms of honesty and accountability. An AI system does not communicate; it generates outputs based on mathematical functions. Calling its output 'testimony' is category error. This remark is obviously in keeping with the trend in the literature identified and discussed here, which attempts to compare the opacity of AI systems with the opacity of human experts. But as we have seen, this comparison collapses under scrutiny because it fails to account for the moral and social dimensions of human expertise. Consider a decision support system as an expert can lead human experts to grant too great a form of agency to the machine, insofar as the latter is in no way competent in the sense that interests us when we speak of expertise—that is, a sense that goes beyond the simple veritistic framework. If we treat an AI system as if it were a peer capable of genuine disagreement, we risk abdicating our own intellectual responsibility. When two peers disagree, they engage in dialogue, challenge each other's reasoning, and seek common ground through argument. An AI system cannot do this. It cannot argue back, nor can it acknowledge the force of counterarguments unless explicitly programmed to simulate such behavior. Even then, simulation is not real engagement. The confusion arises because people conflate correlation with causation or reason with mechanism. They see accurate predictions and assume intelligent agency. But accuracy does not imply understanding, nor does complexity imply virtue. In addition to an individual's consistency in reaching the truth and producing knowledge, other characteristics or qualities are in fact frequently put forward and presented as potentially influencing positively or negatively the trust that people are prepared to place in the person they designate as an expert. Honesty, courage to confront unpopular findings, willingness to update beliefs—all these matter. We would very much like to think our human experts possess such traits, but we also know from experience that even well-intentioned professionals can be swayed by cognitive biases or institutional pressures. Yet, we remain willing to trust them because the framework of social epistemology allows for a degree of deference based on institutional credentials rather than perfect rationality. Extending this model to AI systems requires us to invent entirely new institutions that could certify certain opaque AI technology and approve its use in particular contexts while allowing the technology to remain opaque. Some have proposed such frameworks: imagine a regulatory body auditing algorithmic models for fairness before deployment, issuing certifications akin to medical licenses. But even with such oversight, the core issue remains: can an uninterpretable model ever be said to possess 'epistemic virtues'? If we define expertise strictly in terms of truth-tracking, then perhaps yes. But if we accept that true expertise also involves responsible judgment, ethical reflection, and adaptive learning—qualities that are inherently human—then AI systems cannot genuinely qualify as experts. They can assist, they can inform, they can automate routine tasks. But they cannot stand alone as arbiters of truth or custodians of epistemic values.

In conclusion, my aim in this paper was to assess how far the analogy holds between the trust a layperson places in an expert and the trust—that whether from a layperson or an expert—that a human being places in a deep learning-based artificial system. I began by examining the main elements of the analogy, showing that they rest on the observation that several varieties of opacity are thought to arise in comparable ways in both human experts and AI systems. Given that the opacity of a human expert's actions, decisions, and judgements usually does not prevent lay people from trusting them, this opacity is sometimes invoked to justify placing a similar level of trust in AI systems. But this justification is flawed because it overlooks fundamental differences between human and non-human agents. Human experts operate within a web of social relationships, institutional accountability, and moral responsibility. AI systems operate within a framework of computational processes, data patterns, and engineering constraints. Equating the two is not merely imprecise language; it risks undermining the very foundations of our epistemic practices. If we extend the model of human expertise to AI, we dilute the meaning of both 'expert' and 'trust.' We end up calling machines experts when they lack the essential qualities that make expertise socially valuable: the capacity for growth, the willingness to admit error, the ability to bear responsibility. The fourth section of the paper will show the limits of this conception of expertise, and will argue against conceptualizing trust in artificial systems on the model of trust in human experts. Therefore, modeling the trust we place in AI systems on the trust we place in human experts is a misguided (and potentially epistemically dangerous) attitude. It invites us to rely on tools that cannot be held accountable when they fail, and it blinds us to the ways in which these tools might reinforce existing biases under the guise of neutrality. We must instead develop a separate vocabulary for AI systems—one that acknowledges their utility without exaggerating their autonomy, one that recognizes their opacity as a technical feature rather than an epistemic virtue, and one that preserves our own agency in matters of knowledge and judgment. In doing so, we can harness the power of artificial intelligence without surrendering the moral and intellectual responsibilities that define us as human beings. The future of AI epistemology depends not on pretending that algorithms are peers, but on clearly distinguishing between what they can do—and what they cannot do—in the pursuit of truth and understanding.

Expertise, Opacity, and Trust in AI Systems: A Critique of the Human Analogy

The conjoined issues of the trust placed in experts and the deference shown by non-specialists toward them are well-established topics in social epistemology. Philosophers and sociologists have long examined these dynamics, noting that our reliance on others for knowledge acquisition is a fundamental aspect of human existence. This notion of deference hinges on more general relationships of 'epistemic dependence,' to use John Hardwig's expression, where individuals surrender the full burden of verification to those they deem competent. Traditionally, this dynamic was reserved for human agents: doctors diagnosing illnesses, scientists publishing findings, or legal experts interpreting statutes. However, the landscape of our epistemic world is now being radically redefined by the arrival of artificial systems apparently endowed with entirely new cognitive-like capabilities. These programs are proving to be extremely versatile; depending on the data they are 'trained' on, they can carry out tasks—hitherto exclusively reserved to human experts—with a certain degree of autonomy and in a generally highly satisfactory manner. This shift forces us to revisit traditional matters about trust, dependence, and expertise. We must ask whether these tools function merely as instruments or if they have become bona fide new experts.

The frequently put forward concept of ‘AMA’ for ‘artificial moral agent’ therefore refers to systems that are sufficiently powerful to be put into situations in which they may have to make decisions (in a derivative way) that can have moral consequences (and the systems we are here more particularly interested in can of course fall into this class of artificial moral agents).

Finally, should the relationship between a human expert and an artificial system be seen as a relationship between epistemic peers?

The risk of thinking of artificial systems on the model of human experts (particularly in terms of the trust we place in them) can also take the form of absolving the human agents who use them of their responsibility.

Considering a decision support system as an expert can lead human experts to grant too great a form of agency to the machine, insofar as the latter is in no way competent in the sense that interests us when we speak of expertise – i.e. a sense that goes beyond the simple veritistic framework.

Having argued, as I have done here, that an artificial system cannot be an expert in the same sense that a human being is an expert (and therefore cannot be an epistemic peer to a human expert), one question still remains unanswered: who should be responsible for AI systems’ outputs?

5 Conclusion My aim in this paper was to assess how far the analogy holds between the trust a layperson places in an expert and the trust – whether from a layperson or an expert – that a human being places in a deep learning-based artificial system.
